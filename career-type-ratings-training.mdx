Type ratings control which aircraft families your Career pilot can fly.

In Career Mode, you do not automatically get access to every aircraft. Your airline, contract, training state, and type ratings decide what you can dispatch.

## What a type rating means

A type rating is Career permission to fly an aircraft family.

Examples:

- A320 family.
- 737 family.
- 777 family.
- 787 family.

The exact family names depend on FlyHub's aircraft data.

## Why type ratings matter

Type ratings affect:

- Which jobs you can accept.
- Which airline fleets are useful to you.
- Which Dispatch routes are available.
- Which aircraft appear in training.
- Which aircraft can be assigned in Career My Flights.
- Which future opportunities FlyHub can offer you.

## Starter training

When you begin Career Mode, your first airline can require starter training.

The difficulty changes the training requirement:

| Difficulty | Training flights | Minimum average |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Easy | 5 | 7.0/10 |
| Normal | 10 | 8.0/10 |
| Hard | 15 | 9.0/10 |

If you pass, you earn the type rating and move into normal employment.

If you fail, FlyHub can route you into a recovery path depending on your Career state.

## Market training

Some Job Market offers require training before employment.

The offer and contract show:

- Aircraft family.
- Required training flights.
- Minimum score average.
- Rating price and who pays it.
- Training bond, if the airline pays.
- Training pay rate.
- Base and airline.

Normal market training can pay less than full employment while you complete the program.

## Optional internal type-rating requests

Your current airline can sponsor another type rating, but the path is manual and optional. It is not a random monthly offer.

Your first request becomes available after 100 hours with your current airline. After each internal rating is completed, the next request becomes available after 100 more hours from the hours recorded at completion.

The milestone gives you one request. It does not create a bank of requests. If you reach 200 hours before using the first request, you still have one request available, not two. Waiting never creates extra requests.

### When the request is available

Open **Career > My Company** and look in **Aircraft access**. The qualification panel can be used when:

- You have reached the next 100-hour target with your current airline.
- Your airline operates an aircraft family you do not already hold.
- You do not have another internal request, approved training offer, or type-rating program in progress.

Tenure, recent performance, employer standing, and seniority help the airline prioritize requests when several pilots want the same family. They are shown as career context, not as a hidden random trigger.

### How to request a rating

1. Open **Career > My Company**.
2. Find **Aircraft access** and the **Next type rating** panel.
3. When the panel says **Ready to request**, select **Request a type rating**.
4. Choose one aircraft family that you want to pursue.
5. Select **Send request**.

The airline reviews the request within 24 hours. If the available sponsored slots are full or the request needs another review, it stays active and is checked again within 24 hours. You can see the current status in My Company and in Career notifications.

Possible statuses include:

- **Ready to request** — you can choose a family and send the request.
- **Request under review** — the airline is processing it.
- **Waiting for a fleet opening** — the request remains active for another review.
- **Training offer ready** — the airline approved it; open the offer to begin.
- **Training in progress** — finish the program before requesting another rating.

An internal request is for an extra type you choose yourself, so it is **self-funded**: you pay the rating price when you accept the training offer, and there is no training bond. You keep your current salary and seniority while you train.

For example, a pilot flying the 737 and 777 for a cargo airline can request the Caravan to mix short feeder legs into their flying. The airline does not need them on the Caravan, so the pilot pays for it. See [Who pays for a rating](#who-pays-for-a-rating).

## When your airline retires your aircraft

Airlines change their fleets. If your airline stops flying every aircraft family you are rated on (for example, it retires the 757 and you only fly the 757), you are not left stuck:

- **Free transition type rating.** As soon as the schedule update that retires your aircraft goes live, the airline sends you a training offer it pays for in full on one of its current aircraft. It shows up in your notifications and in the Job Market. Pick the type you want. You keep your pay, rank and seniority, and it does not use up your 100-hour request.
- **Leave at no cost.** While you have no aircraft to fly at your airline, you can resign or accept another airline's offer with no early exit penalty, no reputation hit and no bonus repayment.
- **No inactivity penalty.** Months where you could not fly because of the fleet change do not count against you.

If you decline or let the transition offer expire, it comes back 7 days later: open **Career > My Company** and it will be waiting while you are still without a flyable aircraft.

## Type-rating price

A type rating is priced in **hours of pay** that grow more slowly than your pay. A 5-star Captain pays more dollars for a rating than a 1-star First Officer, but fewer hours of their own flying.

| Rating | Listed hours |
| --- | ---: |
| Narrowbody, turboprop or regional type | 30 h |
| Widebody type | 45 h |
| Differences course (you hold a family that shares training) | 25% of the above |

Widebody types include the A300, A310, A330, A340, A350, A380, 747, 767, 777, 787, MD-11, DC-10 and L-1011 families. Everything else uses the 30-hour price.

At $200/h you pay exactly the listed hours. Above that you pay fewer hours of your own pay, below it more: earning 4× more costs 2× the dollars. Prices are rounded to $10. It always uses your full contract rate, even while you are on training pay. Between jobs it uses the rate of your last contract, so resigning does not make anything cheaper. A pilot who has never had a contract pays the lowest rate in the game.

### Differences courses

Some families share most of their training. If you already hold a **current** rating on one family in a group, the next one in the same group is a **differences course** at 25% of the normal price. An expired rating does not count until you restore it with recurrency. Job Market cards, offers and the type request list show the saving, for example "Differences course: 9.1 h of pay instead of 36.4 h".

- Airbus fly-by-wire: A320, A330, A340, A350 and A380.
- A300 and A310.
- 757 and 767.
- 777 and 787.
- Embraer E-Jet and ERJ.
- MD-80, MD-90, DC-9 and 717.

### Examples

| Rating | At $104/h | At $305/h |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Narrowbody (30 h listed) | $4,330 (41.6 h of your pay) | $7,410 (24.3 h) |
| Widebody (45 h listed) | $6,490 (62.4 h) | $11,110 (36.4 h) |
| Narrowbody differences (7.5 h listed) | $1,080 (10.4 h) | $1,850 (6.1 h) |
| Widebody differences (11.25 h listed) | $1,620 (15.6 h) | $2,780 (9.1 h) |

The offer always shows the exact price, in dollars and in hours of your pay.

## Who pays for a rating

| Situation | Who pays | Training bond |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A new-hire seat that needs a new type, including Job Market offers | Airline, 100% | Yes, 26 weeks |
| A transition after your airline retires your fleet | Airline, 100% | Yes, 26 weeks |
| A type offer sent to you by airline operations | Airline, 100% | Yes, 26 weeks |
| A promotion that moves you to a new type | Airline, 100% | Yes, 26 weeks |
| An extra type you ask for with an internal request | You | No |
| Your first job (starter training) | Included | No |

### The training bond

When the airline pays for your rating, you sign a **26-week training bond** for that amount. The bond is printed on your contract, and offers show it before you accept.

The 26 weeks start on the day your rating is awarded, not the day you sign. If you leave or are dismissed while you are still in training, you owe nothing for the bond.

After the award, if you leave before the bond ends, you repay the share for the weeks that are left. Leave 13 weeks in and you repay about half. My Company shows what is left on the bond and the date it ends.

| How you leave | Bond repayment |
| --- | --- |
| You resign | Yes, for the weeks left |
| You leave for another airline | Yes, for the weeks left |
| You are dismissed for strikes or inactivity | Yes, for the weeks left |
| Your airline shuts down | Nothing |
| Your airline retires your fleet | Nothing |
| You are recalled through the union | Nothing |
| The airline ends your contract for its own reasons | Nothing |

The resignation dialog in My Company and the contract step of an outside offer show the repayment before you confirm. It appears in the ledger as **Training bond repayment**. After 26 weeks, the bond is done and nothing is owed. You get a **Training bond cleared** notification when that happens.

Some outside offers include **We buy out your training bond** in the hiring package. If you accept one, the new airline pays what you still owe and you are charged nothing for the bond. Bigger airlines offer this more often.

Before accepting training, check:

- The rating price, in dollars and hours.
- Who pays, and whether a bond applies.
- Your balance, if you pay it yourself.
- Training pay.
- Required performance average.

## During training

Training changes what Dispatch shows.

While you are training on a type, Dispatch focuses on that training family. Even if you already have other type ratings, FlyHub can limit visible dispatch work until the training program is complete.

This keeps the training contract clean and prevents unrelated line work from interfering.

## Passing training

To pass training:

1. Sign the training contract.
2. Complete the required training flights.
3. Keep the average score above the contract minimum.
4. Wait for FlyHub to process the training result.
5. Sign the employment or updated contract if required.

After passing, the type rating appears in your Career profile and the related aircraft family can become available when your airline fleet supports it.

## Failing training

Failing training can have consequences.

Depending on the training path, you can:

- Lose the offer.
- Return to a previous line contract.
- Become unemployed.
- Receive a recovery offer.
- Need to find another job path through Job Market.

Always read the contract before starting training. The contract tells you the score requirement and what you are agreeing to.

## Recurrent training and currency

A purchased rating stays current when you fly that aircraft family at least once every 90 days.

If a rating expires, it is not removed. You can restore it by flying the family again or by completing simulator recurrency.

Simulator recurrency costs 10% of each expired rating's current price. One session restores all expired ratings, and the whole session is capped at **8 listed hours of pay**. The recurrency dialog shows the cap in dollars. An offer quotes recurrency before joining at your own pay rate, the same amount you are charged. Reinstating a rating before you join an airline that flies it uses the same price. Retraining for aircraft families operated by your current airline is free.

Example: at $104 an hour the session cap is $830. At $305 an hour it is $2,440. SOP Discipline and Checklist Discipline lower the fee further.

Line checks and medical checks are separate from normal type-rating training. If you miss required checks or fail a line check, you can be grounded until the issue is fixed.

For compliance rules, see [Career Compliance](/career-compliance).

## Common mistakes

- Leaving an airline early without checking what is left on a training bond.
- Requesting an extra type without checking its self-funded price.
- Forgetting that training can limit Dispatch to one aircraft family.
- Assuming a type rating is useful at every airline.
- Ignoring the required average score.
- Opening an offer and letting the timer expire.
- Forgetting that Career scoring affects training outcomes.
